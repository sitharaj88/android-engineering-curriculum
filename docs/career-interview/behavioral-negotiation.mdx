# Behavioral Interviews & Negotiation

The technical rounds get you to the offer table. The **behavioral round
and negotiation** determine the offer level and compensation. Most
candidates underprepare for both — and leave $20k-$100k on the table.
This chapter covers preparation, delivery, and the four negotiation
moves.

## Behavioral interview structure

Most companies run one 45-60 minute behavioral round. Typical questions:

- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate."
- "Tell me about a project you owned end-to-end."
- "Tell me about a time you missed a deadline."
- "Tell me about a technical decision you made that turned out wrong."
- "Tell me about a time you mentored someone."
- "Tell me about a time you influenced without authority."
- "Tell me about a system you designed."

Different from "what's your favorite Kotlin feature?" — these probe
how you actually **work** with people, ambiguity, and failure.

---

## STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result

Structure every answer:

```text
SITUATION (15-20 seconds)
  Set the scene. Where, when, who, scale.

TASK (10 seconds)
  Your specific responsibility.

ACTION (60-90 seconds — the meat)
  What YOU did. Specifically. Avoid "we did" — interviewer wants YOUR
  contribution.

RESULT (15-20 seconds)
  Measurable outcome. Lesson learned.
```

Total: ~2-3 minutes per story. Practice timing.

### Example — disagreement

> **S**: At my previous role, I was leading the Android team at X. We
> were 3 weeks out from a major launch, and the iOS lead proposed
> changing the auth response shape for parity with their app. It would
> have required us to rewrite our session-management code.
>
> **T**: I had to decide whether to absorb the breaking change late, or
> push back.
>
> **A**: I scheduled a 30-minute meeting with the iOS lead + the backend
> lead. I came with three options written up: keep both shapes (cost: $X
> backend), Android adapts (cost: 5 dev-days + risk to launch), iOS
> adapts (cost: 3 dev-days, no risk). We talked through each. I argued
> for option 3 based on the timeline + the fact that our session code
> had been audited just a month earlier. The iOS lead agreed once they
> saw the timeline math.
>
> **R**: We shipped on time. The iOS rewrite happened in the following
> sprint with no incidents. I added "schedule cross-team alignment for
> any auth schema changes" to our team's checklist — never had this
> debate again.

Specific, measurable, you-driven, with a lesson.

You won't get asked all of these, but you should have one prepared
for each:

## Common pitfalls

### "We" instead of "I"

> "We decided to refactor the architecture..."

Interviewer can't tell what **you** specifically did. Use "I" liberally
— with attribution where appropriate ("I worked with the backend team
on this, but I owned the Android side").

### Story that's actually about something else

Interviewer asks about disagreement. You answer with a story about a
deadline. **Listen carefully** — answer the question asked. If your
story doesn't fit, pick a different one.

### Too long / no result

A 6-minute story bores the interviewer. The **Result** is what makes the
story memorable. End strong.

### No lesson

The follow-up question after every story: "What would you do
differently?" Have an answer. Even great outcomes have improvement
opportunities.

### Negative about previous employer / coworker

Even when justified, don't. Interviewers note candidates who blame
others. Frame challenges neutrally:

> ❌ "My old manager was a micromanager who didn't trust me."
>
> ✓ "I had ambitious goals on the Android side that required more
> autonomy than my team had been given. I worked with my manager to
> earn that trust by..."

## Leveling-specific signals

Interviewers grade against the level you're being considered for. Hit
the **scope signals** the level expects.

### L4 → L5 (Senior)

- Owns a **feature or subsystem** end-to-end
- Influences **the team's** technical direction
- Mentors juniors
- Resolves disagreements **within the team**

Stories should show team-level scope. "I designed the auth subsystem
that the team uses for all flows."

### L5 → L6 (Staff)

- Owns a **cross-team initiative or platform**
- Influences **the org's** technical direction
- Mentors seniors across teams
- Resolves disagreements **between teams**
- Identifies what should be built (not just executes)

Stories should show cross-team / cross-org scope. "I noticed every team
was rebuilding their own auth caching layer. I proposed a shared library,
got buy-in from 4 teams, drove the rollout over 6 months."

### L6 → L7 (Principal)

- Owns **multi-org or company-level** technical strategy
- Influences hiring, organizational structure
- Speaks externally (conferences, blog, OSS)
- Resolves ambiguity at the **company level**

Stories should show strategic / external impact. "I wrote the company's
mobile architecture vision document, which became the basis for the
2024 platform strategy across 12 teams."

If you're interviewing for L6 with L5-scope stories, the interviewer
codes you as "not yet ready." Match scope deliberately.

## Reverse questions — the underrated half

The interviewer always asks: "any questions for me?"

**Good questions** signal seriousness and discernment. Have 3-5 prepared
per interview round, tailored to the role.

### Strong questions

- "What's the most ambiguous decision the team is wrestling with right now?"
- "How does the team handle disagreement about technical direction?"
- "What did the last person who left this role do well? What was missing?"
- "How does the team measure success for an engineer at L6?"
- "What's the current crash-free rate? ANR rate? How are you trying to improve?"
- "How does the team balance shipping features vs paying down tech debt?"
- "Who would I work with most closely?"
- "What's your favorite thing about the team? Most frustrating thing?"

### Avoid

- "What's the company's mission?" (read the website)
- "What's the salary range?" (save for recruiter)
- "Do you offer remote work?" (research first, ask if unclear)
- Anything that suggests you didn't prepare

## Salary negotiation

Most engineers leave **$10k-$100k on the table** by accepting the first
offer. The hiring process is heavily weighted toward the company; the
negotiation is your only leverage moment.

### The four moves

#### 1. Never give a number first

When a recruiter asks "what are your expectations?":

> "I'd like to find a role that's a great fit; I'm sure we can align on
> compensation once we know it's a match. Can you share the band for
> this level?"

Or if pressed:

> "I'm targeting competitive senior compensation for the New York market.
> Happy to align on specifics once I see the components."

If you state a number, it caps the offer. Companies have ranges; let
them anchor.

#### 2. Get competing offers

The single biggest leverage move. Two real offers = 15-30% upside.

If you have an active interview at Company A and want to use it as
leverage:

> "I should mention I'm in final rounds at Company X — they've
> indicated they'd like to move quickly. Would it be possible to align
> on timeline?"

Don't lie. Recruiters check. But genuine competing offers reset
negotiation entirely.

#### 3. Negotiate components separately

Compensation isn't one number — it's:
- **Base salary**
- **Sign-on bonus** (1-time)
- **Annual bonus** (target %)
- **Equity** (RSUs / stock options / amount + vesting + cliff)
- **Equity refreshers** (annual additional grant)
- **Benefits** (401k match, healthcare, learning budget)

Each is independently negotiable. If they won't budge on base, push
sign-on or refreshers. Sign-on bonuses are easier to grant — they're
1-time and don't compound.

#### 4. Take 24 hours before signing

"This is exciting. Let me discuss with my family. I'll get back to you
tomorrow."

24-hour pause does three things:
- Lets you sleep on it (clarity)
- Signals you're considering options
- Gives time for a competing offer to materialize

Recruiters won't withdraw an offer over 24 hours. Anyone who would is
not a company you want to work for.

## What to actually say

### After verbal offer

> "Thank you, that's exciting. Before we go further, can you walk me
> through the components — base, bonus, equity, refresh policy?"

Get the full picture in writing or at least verbally complete.

### Counter-offer

> "I appreciate the offer. Based on my research and the level of role,
> I was hoping for [number] on base. Is there flexibility there?"

Be specific. Vague counters get vague responses. Anchor with research
(levels.fyi, glassdoor, your network).

### If they say no on base

> "I understand. Could we look at a higher sign-on bonus and/or
> additional RSU refresh to bridge the gap?"

Components matter. Sign-on doesn't constrain future raises. RSU refresh
compounds.

### If they say yes

> "That works. Could you send me an updated offer in writing? I'll
> have a decision within 24 hours."

Get it in writing before celebrating. Verbal offers are non-binding.

### If they push back on the 24 hours

> "I take all offers seriously, and I want to ensure I'm making a
> decision I can fully commit to. 24 hours lets me review carefully and
> come back with a confident yes."

Hold firm. Reputable recruiters respect this.

## Compensation expectations (2025)

Approximate ranges (USD, US-coastal markets — Bay, NY, Seattle):

| Level | Big tech (FAANG-ish) | Unicorn / pre-IPO | Startup / SMB |
|-------|----------------------|-------------------|---------------|
| L3 (Junior) | $200-260k TC | $160-220k TC | $130-180k TC |
| L4 (Mid) | $290-400k TC | $230-340k TC | $180-260k TC |
| L5 (Senior) | $400-600k TC | $310-500k TC | $250-380k TC |
| L6 (Staff) | $580-900k TC | $440-720k TC | $370-580k TC |
| L7+ (Principal) | $750k-1.5M+ TC | varies | varies |

Total comp = base + bonus + RSUs/4. Equity often vests over 4 years.

Smaller markets (Austin, Denver, Boston, Pittsburgh, Toronto): typically
10-25% lower. Remote-friendly companies pay closer to the high tier in
all locations.

International (India / Europe): wildly different. Bangalore senior IC
~₹50-90 LPA TC (~$60-110k USD), London £80-130k base + equity. Adjust by
local market, not US dollars.

### Where to research

- **levels.fyi** — most comprehensive
- **glassdoor.com** — older but useful
- **blind** (TeamBlind) — anonymous tea
- **Your network** — most accurate, hardest to access

## Compensation evaluation framework

Don't optimize purely for total comp. Score offers on:

| Factor                       | Weight |
|------------------------------|--------|
| Total comp (1st year)        | 30%    |
| Total comp (4-year vest)     | 25%    |
| Role / scope / growth        | 20%    |
| Team / manager               | 10%    |
| Company stability + trajectory | 10%   |
| Location / lifestyle         | 5%     |

A 20% higher comp at a company you'll quit in 8 months is worse than a
slightly lower offer with multi-year upside.

### Risk-adjusted equity

Private company equity is **lottery tickets** — value depends on the
liquidity event. Risk-adjust by company stage:

- **Pre-Series A** — assume zero. Salary must justify.
- **Series B-C** — 30-50% expected realization.
- **Series D+ / pre-IPO** — 60-90%.
- **Public** — face value.

A 1-year offer of $300k base + $500k pre-IPO RSU might be worse than
$280k base + $300k public RSU, depending on the company.

## Edge cases

### Lowball offer

Sometimes the first offer is far below market. Two options:
- Counter aggressively (e.g., +25-40%). They expected to negotiate.
- Walk away if the gap is too large; they don't value your level.

### "Best and final"

Companies sometimes claim no more flexibility. Sometimes true; often
not. Counter once more, gently:

> "I appreciate the offer being your best. To make this decisive for me,
> would it be possible to add [specific item]?"

If they hold firm, you decide whether to accept or decline. They've
shown their hand.

### Resignation conversation

After accepting, you resign at your current job. Expect a counter-offer
50%+ of the time. **Don't accept** — research shows engineers who
counter-accept leave within a year anyway, and your manager will
remember you almost left.

> "I appreciate the counter, but my decision is made. Happy to discuss a
> handover plan."

## Key takeaways

<Callout kind="success" title="Behavioral & negotiation rules">
- **STAR structure** — Situation, Task, Action, Result. 2-3 min per story.
- **8-10 stories prepared** — disagreement, ownership, failure, mentor, influence, etc.
- **Use "I" not "we"** — interviewer wants your contribution.
- **Match scope** to the level you're interviewing for.
- **Prepare reverse questions** — they signal seriousness.
- **Never give a number first** — let them anchor.
- **Negotiate components separately** — base + sign-on + equity + refreshers.
- **Take 24 hours** before signing. Always.
- **Research with levels.fyi + network** — not glassdoor alone.
</Callout>
